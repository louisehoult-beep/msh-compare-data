Selling health tech into the NHS: the approval gates

Before a trust or Integrated Care Board (ICB) can buy a digital product, an Artificial Intelligence (AI) tool or software as a medical device (SaMD), it has to pass a set of regulatory and assurance gates. This is the order they come in, what changed in 2026, and the questions to ask the customer at each one.

01
Regulator

MHRA registration, and which marking is recognised

Every medical device, including software that qualifies as one, has to be registered with the MHRA before it can be placed on the market in Great Britain (England, Wales and Scotland). Registration is the starting line, not an approval.

  • Registration comes first. All medical devices, including in vitro diagnostic medical devices (IVDs), must be registered with the MHRA before they are placed on the market in Great Britain, and the registration must be kept up while the device is still available.
  • Registration is not approval. The MHRA states that registration is not any form of accreditation, certification, approval or endorsement.
  • Non-UK manufacturers must appoint a single UK Responsible Person. Accounts of former Great Britain-based Authorised Representatives that had not switched to the UK Responsible Person role by 30 March 2026 are now closed.
  • Public register. Registered manufacturers and their device types are listed on the Public Access Registration Database (PARD). Device brand or trade names have been displayed there from 23 February 2026.
  • The Great Britain marking. UK Conformity Assessed (UKCA) is the Great Britain marking. It is not recognised in Northern Ireland, where a Conformité Européenne (CE) or CE UKNI marking is needed.
  • New, 21 October 2026: CE marking will be recognised long term. The government confirmed that CE marked medical devices and IVDs will continue to be accepted in Great Britain on a long-term basis, with no additional UK approval route for devices already approved for the EU market. The MHRA consultation ran from 16 February to 10 April 2026 and received 1,056 responses. More than 90% of devices registered for use in Great Britain carry a CE mark.
  • Which CE devices. All CE marked devices certified under the EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR) and In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Regulation (IVDR) will be recognised indefinitely. Devices certified under the older Medical Device Directive (MDD) will be accepted until the end of the EU transition period, currently 31 December 2028, instead of 30 June 2028. MDD devices placed compliantly on the EU or Great Britain market before then can stay on the Great Britain market. The proposed separate reliance route for devices classed higher in Great Britain than in the EU will not go ahead.
  • Not law yet. The MHRA will bring in the legislation, subject to parliamentary approval, before 30 June 2028. Until it is made, the dates in current MHRA guidance are unchanged: MDD or Active Implantable Medical Devices Directive (AIMDD) devices until the sooner of certificate expiry or 30 June 2028; In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Directive (IVDD) devices until the sooner of certificate expiry or 30 June 2030; MDR or IVDR devices until 30 June 2030.
  • Domestic duties stay. Manufacturers of CE marked devices must still appoint a UK Responsible Person, register with the MHRA and meet post-market surveillance obligations. The MHRA stays responsible for the safety of every device on the UK market, whatever its route, including through the Yellow Card scheme.
  • UKCA stays. UKCA remains the domestic approval route in Great Britain and is being modernised through the pre-market statutory instrument. The government will also seek powers through primary legislation for a future licensing approach, including for software and AI medical devices.
  • Not verifiedAIMDD and IVDD devices. The government response does not mention them. Check the MHRA’s timelines page before quoting a date for either.

What the rep needs to know

  • Never say “MHRA approved” about a registration. It is a legal requirement to be on the market, nothing more.
  • Customers will ask about the 21 October decision. MDR and IVDR certified CE devices will be recognised indefinitely once the law changes, and MDD devices move to 31 December 2028. It is not law yet.
  • Know your product’s marking basis (MDR, IVDR or an older directive) and its certificate expiry date.
  • If your manufacturer is outside the UK, know who your UK Responsible Person is. Buyers can see it on the public register.

Ask the customer

  • What marking evidence does your procurement or medical devices team need on file: the UKCA declaration, the CE certificate, or both?
  • Do you check certificate expiry dates at contract award and renewal?
  • Do any of your sites or partners sit in Northern Ireland?

02
Regulator

Software and AI regulation: the Airlock and the Commission

In the UK many software and AI products are regulated as medical devices or as in vitro diagnostic devices. The rules for AI are now being rebuilt, and the timetable matters to anyone selling an AI product into a trust.

  • The MHRA’s software and AI guidance includes guiding principles on Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCPs), for managing rapid product changes, and on transparency for machine-learning devices. The page was last updated 3 February 2025.
  • The National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare was set up by the MHRA in September 2025 as an independent, non-statutory body. It published its recommendations on 10 September 2026 after engagement with more than 12,000 people.
  • On 6 October 2026 the government accepted all 44 recommendations. Its stated next steps: MHRA draft guidance on managing changes to AI-enabled devices by December 2026; a consultation on how to qualify and classify AI-enabled devices beginning in 2027; exploring staged authorisation, so promising tools can be used earlier under close supervision while real-world evidence is gathered; and a full implementation roadmap by Spring 2027.
  • The AI Airlock is the MHRA’s regulatory sandbox for AI as a Medical Device (AIaMD). The pilot closed in April 2025 with four products. Phase 2 completed in May 2026 with seven innovators across three regulatory challenges. The Department of Health and Social Care (DHSC) allocated £1.2 million a year for 2026 to 2029.
  • Phase 3 opened on 6 October 2026. Its priority is post-market surveillance and oversight across the product lifecycle. Applications stay open year-round and are reviewed in waves: apply before 31 October 2026 for the current wave, with the first wave selected in November 2026 and the next shortlisting in Spring 2027. Testing usually runs 6 to 12 months, there is no fee, and the phase is funded until April 2029.
  • The Airlock is not a route to market. The MHRA describes it as a way to test regulatory challenges and de-risk products ahead of seeking market entry. Phase 3 participants will be listed on the Airlock page once onboarded; none are listed yet.

What the rep needs to know

  • Buyers will increasingly ask how an AI tool is monitored after go-live. That is the theme of both the Commission response and Airlock Phase 3.
  • Know whether your product has a change control plan for algorithm updates, and what the customer will be told when the model changes.
  • Dates to watch: draft change guidance by December 2026, the classification consultation in 2027, the roadmap by Spring 2027.

Ask the customer

  • Who signs off AI tools here? Is there a digital, AI or clinical governance group, and when does it next meet?
  • How will you want to monitor the tool’s performance once it is live, and what data do you need from us for that?
  • If the software updates its model, what does your change process require before the update goes live?

Sources, read 20 October 2026: MHRA, Software and AI as a medical device; Government backs recommendations of NHS doctors-led AI Commission, 6 October 2026; AI Airlock collection (updated 6 October 2026); AI Airlock Phase 3 application; funding announcement, 8 April 2026.

03
NHS assurance

DTAC: the refreshed form, and the old one retired

The Digital Technology Assessment Criteria (DTAC) is the assessment framework that care commissioners and providers use to assure digital health products for the NHS and adult social care. It is the gate most digital buyers ask about first.

  • Five areas: clinical safety, data protection, technical security, interoperability, and usability and accessibility.
  • It sits alongside other approvals, not instead of them. Products may still need medical device certification or registration with the Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO). DTAC is also used alongside the Data Security and Protection Toolkit (DSPT) and the pre-acquisition questionnaire for medical devices.
  • Refreshed in February 2026: a new form with 25% fewer questions, de-duplicated with the DSPT and the pre-acquisition questionnaire; clearer guidance on purpose, scope and completion; and scope aligned with NICE, focusing DTAC on software-based digital health technologies.
  • The previous form should not be used from 6 April 2026. NHS England asks users to download the form at the point of use so they have the current version.
  • More change is coming. Reviews of some standards within DTAC, including DCB0129 and DCB0160, are still ongoing, and NHS England says it will update DTAC as they change. The page was last edited 21 September 2026.
  • No central list. The NHS England DTAC page does not publish a register of products that have passed. A claim that a product is “DTAC approved” cannot be checked against a national list.

What the rep needs to know

  • If your company’s DTAC pack was completed before February 2026, it is on the old form. Get it redone on the current form before you send it.
  • Each buyer assesses DTAC for themselves. Another trust’s sign-off helps the conversation but does not replace this customer’s assessment.
  • Expect the same questions from information governance, digital and clinical safety leads. Know who in your company answers each part.

Ask the customer

  • Who owns the DTAC assessment here: digital, information governance or clinical safety?
  • Do you want the completed form before a pilot, or only before a contract?
  • Will you accept an assessment already completed by another NHS organisation as a starting point?

Source, read 20 October 2026: NHS England Digital, DTAC guidance for buyers and suppliers (last edited 21 September 2026).

04
Clinical safety

DCB0129 and DCB0160: one for you, one for the customer

These are the two clinical risk management standards for health information technology (IT) systems. Compliance with both is mandatory under the Health and Social Care Act 2012.

  • Status: both are accepted for publication by the Data Coordination Board (DCB) under section 250 of the Health and Social Care Act 2012. Each has a specification and implementation guidance.
  • DCB0129 sets clinical risk management requirements for manufacturers of health IT systems. This is your company’s obligation.
  • DCB0160 requires a health organisation to set up a framework in which the clinical risks of deploying a new or changed health IT system are properly managed. This is the customer’s obligation.
  • Under review. NHS England says reviews of DCB0129 and DCB0160 are ongoing. The standards page itself was last edited 11 October 2024, and no revised versions or dates have been published.

What the rep needs to know

  • The customer cannot finish DCB0160 without your DCB0129 evidence. Have it ready before the first technical meeting.
  • Deployment speed depends on the customer’s own clinical safety resource. Find out early how stretched it is.
  • A product change can reopen the customer’s clinical risk work, so tell them about updates before they land.

Ask the customer

  • Who is your clinical safety lead for this deployment?
  • What do you need from our DCB0129 documentation to complete your DCB0160 work?
  • How long does your clinical safety review usually take, and what holds it up?

Sources, read 20 October 2026: NHS England Digital, Clinical risk management standards; DTAC guidance (reviews ongoing).

05
Data security

The DSPT: your company’s status is public

The DSPT is an online self-assessment against the National Data Guardian’s 10 data security standards. Every organisation with access to NHS patient data and systems must use it, suppliers included.

  • Who must complete it: all organisations that have access to NHS patient data and systems.
  • Anyone can look you up. The public Organisation Search shows the status of an organisation’s most recent self-assessment, filters by sector, including IT suppliers, and downloads the results as a spreadsheet.
  • The 2026-27 version 9 is out. The outcomes, assertions and evidence items were published on 2 October 2026, and the areas of mandatory audit for 2026-27, which include IT suppliers, on 1 October 2026.
  • It links to DTAC. The refreshed DTAC form was de-duplicated against the DSPT, so a current DSPT submission saves work in the DTAC assessment.

What the rep needs to know

  • Check your own company’s published status before the customer does. A lapsed or unmet status will be found.
  • Know which year and version your company’s latest submission covers, and when the next one is due.

Ask the customer

  • Will you check our published DSPT status, or do you need a copy of our submission?
  • Does your information governance team need anything beyond the DSPT for this product, such as a data protection impact assessment?

Sources, read 20 October 2026: Data Security and Protection Toolkit; DSPT Organisation Search.

Gates 1 to 5 get you in the door

Gates 6 to 9 are about evidence and money: whether the NHS has judged the product worth buying, and who pays.

06
Evidence

The NICE HealthTech programme: early use and existing use

NICE HealthTech guidance evaluates digital technologies, diagnostics and medical devices, including AI. Since July 2025 it runs as one programme with two main kinds of assessment.

  • One programme. The NICE HealthTech programme combines the former Diagnostics Assessment, Interventional Procedures and Medical Technologies Evaluation programmes. Its manual was published on 14 July 2025, last updated 17 December 2025, and applies to all guidance started after 14 July 2025.
  • Early-use guidance (previously called early value assessment) rapidly assesses products early in their lifecycle that already have the right regulatory approval for UK use, or have limited NHS use and need more evidence. They can be conditionally recommended while further evidence is generated.
  • Existing-use guidance (previously called late-stage assessment) assesses groups of similar technologies already in established NHS use, to support procurement and commissioning. It can recommend one technology over similar options and can look at whether price differences are justified.
  • The Evidence Standards Framework sets evidence standards for digital health technologies. NICE says commissioners and evaluators use it when deciding whether to commission one.

What the rep needs to know

  • Find out whether your product, or its category, has HealthTech guidance and what kind. A conditional early-use recommendation comes with evidence generation, not unconditional adoption.
  • Existing-use guidance compares you with competitors already in the NHS, including on price. Know where your product sits in it.
  • Map your evidence against the Evidence Standards Framework before a commissioner does.

Ask the customer

  • Does your team look for NICE guidance before buying in this category, and is a conditional early-use recommendation enough?
  • Would you consider being an evidence-generation site?
  • Which evidence does your commissioning or digital board weigh most?

Sources, read 20 October 2026: NICE, About HealthTech guidance; NICE HealthTech programme manual (PMG48), chapters 3 and 4; Evidence Standards Framework for digital health technologies.

07
National funding

The National HealthTech Access Programme

A new route that funds a small number of high-impact technologies nationally, the way medicines are funded. It is selective: most products will not go through it.

  • Launched 9 February 2026 (previously called the Rules Based Pathway). It is run by NICE with DHSC, NHS England, the MHRA and the Office for Life Sciences, and expands NICE’s technology appraisal approach to health technologies.
  • National funding. Technologies recommended through it receive national NHS funding and are made available across the health service. NICE says it covers England and Wales.
  • First two topics: capsule sponge tests for detecting oesophageal cancer, and AI tools that interpret tissue-sample images for suspected prostate and breast cancer.
  • Two further topics referred subject to more evidence: technologies to improve detection of endometrial cancer in women with unexplained vaginal bleeding, and AI to help analyse chest X-rays for suspected lung cancer in primary care referrals.

What the rep needs to know

  • If you sell in one of these four areas, a national decision is coming that may change how your customers buy, whether you are in the topic or competing with it.
  • Outside these topics, there is no national funding route for your product through this programme yet.

Ask the customer

  • Are you holding back local decisions in this area until the national evaluation reports?
  • If national funding comes through, what would change in how you buy or roll out?

Sources, read 20 October 2026: NICE news, 9 February 2026; NICE, About HealthTech guidance.

08
Local funding

The MedTech Funding Mandate is frozen for 2026/27

The mandate has supported NICE-recommended, cost-saving technologies since 1 April 2021. It stays live, but nothing new is being added this year.

  • No new products for 2026/27. NHS England is reviewing the policy against the 10 Year Health Plan and the Life Sciences Sector Plan. During the review the policy remains live, but no new products will be added for 2026/27.
  • How it is funded. Supported technologies are typically funded by commissioners from their existing allocations.
  • The criteria, as NHS England’s page reads on 20 October 2026: effectiveness shown through positive NICE Medical Technology Guidance or Diagnostics Guidance; a net saving within three years; and a national budget impact of no more than £20 million in any of the first three years.

What the rep needs to know

  • Do not promise mandate support for a new product in 2026/27. It is not available.
  • Products already supported stay supported while the policy is live. Check NHS England’s page for the current list before naming one.

Ask the customer

  • Without the mandate, which budget would this come from: the trust, the ICB or a transformation fund?
  • What saving, and over what period, would you need to see to fund it locally?

Source, read 20 October 2026: NHS England, MedTech funding mandate and support.

09
Innovation pathway

IDAP: a closed pilot, not an open route

The Innovative Devices Access Pathway (IDAP) was set up to speed up innovative devices that meet an unmet clinical need in the NHS. The pilot is closed and no new round has been announced.

  • Pilot closed. Eight applicants were selected for the pilot in February 2024. The pilot phase and its patient and public engagement are marked closed.
  • Partners: DHSC, Health Technology Wales (HTW), the MHRA, NHS England, NICE, the Office for Life Sciences (OLS) and the Scottish Health Technologies Group (SHTG), part of Healthcare Improvement Scotland.
  • A narrow exception. An Unmet Clinical Need Authorisation (UCNA) lets a device without UKCA or CE marking be supplied in Great Britain in the interest of public health, under conditions. The MHRA states it is not an alternative route to the UK market and applies only to IDAP pilot products.
  • One UCNA was issued in the pilot, for a histotripsy tumour treatment system. Its latest extension runs to 30 October 2026.
  • What comes next: the MHRA says the pilot is testing the elements of a future IDAP. No dates for a next round are published on the page as at 20 October 2026.

What the rep needs to know

  • IDAP is not a route open to your product now. Do not present it as one.
  • Watch the MHRA’s IDAP page for a next round and for the UCNA expiry on 30 October 2026.

Ask the customer

  • Are you involved in any national innovation pilots in this specialty, and how do they affect what you buy?

Sources, read 20 October 2026: MHRA, The Innovative Devices Access Pathway (updated 30 April 2026), with its pages on the pilot phase and on the UCNA.

Gate 10

Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland

The regulator’s rules cover Great Britain, but most NHS assurance gates on this page are written for England. Check the nation before you assume a gate applies.

Scotland

Applies: MHRA registration and UKCA or recognised CE marking, as part of Great Britain.

AI policy: the Scottish Health Secretary said on 6 October 2026 that the Scottish Government has interim guidance on AI in health and social care and that an AI policy framework is due later this year.

National advice: SHTG, part of Healthcare Improvement Scotland, was an IDAP partner.

Not verifiedScotland’s own assessment process for digital products. The SHTG site refused our check; ask the health board’s digital lead which national advice it follows.

Wales

Applies: MHRA registration and UKCA or recognised CE marking, as part of Great Britain.

National funding: NICE says the National HealthTech Access Programme covers England and Wales.

National advice: Health Technology Wales was an IDAP partner.

Not verifiedWhether Welsh health boards use DTAC or their own digital assurance. Ask the health board.

Northern Ireland

Marking: UKCA is not recognised. Devices need a CE or CE UKNI marking. The government says indefinite CE recognition in Great Britain lets manufacturers place the same product on the whole UK market.

AI: Northern Ireland’s Health Minister welcomed the Commission’s recommendations on 6 October 2026.
